# Step 3: Collaborating with Editors

In this step you will:
1. Invite an editor to your repository
2. Understand what permissions to give them
3. Use comments to discuss specific lines
4. Review and accept suggested changes

## Part A: Invite your editor

1. Open your repository on github.com.
2. Click **Settings** (near the top of the repository page).
3. In the left sidebar, click **Collaborators**.
4. Click **Add people**.
5. Type your editor's GitHub username or email address.
6. Select your editor from the results, then click **Add collaborator**.
7. Choose a permission level:
   - **Read** — can look at files but cannot change anything. Good for a proofreader who only needs to read.
   - **Triage** — can manage issues and comments but cannot change files.
   - **Write** — can upload, edit, and delete files. Good for an editor who will make direct changes.
   - **Maintain** — can manage the repository but not delete it.
   - **Admin** — full control, including deleting the repository.
8. Click **Add collaborator** to finish.

Your editor will receive an email invitation. They must accept it before they can access your repository.

## What permission should you choose?

| Editor role | Recommended permission |
|-------------|------------------------|
| Proofreader (reads only) | Read |
| Editor who leaves comments | Triage or Write |
| Editor who rewrites text directly | Write |
| Co-author who helps manage the project | Maintain |

Start with the lowest permission that lets your editor do their job. You can change it later.

## Part B: Leave comments on specific lines

Instead of emailing your editor a list like "page 5, paragraph 2, fix this sentence," you can comment right on the text.

1. Open your manuscript file on github.com.
2. Click the line number next to the text you want to comment on. A blue comment icon appears.
3. Click the comment icon and type your comment, such as:
   > This paragraph feels slow. Can we tighten it?
4. Click **Add single comment**.

Your editor can reply to your comment, resolve it when it is addressed, or start a new comment thread elsewhere in the file.

## Part C: Review changes your editor made

If your editor has Write access and makes changes:

1. Open your repository and click **Insights**, then **Network** (or click your file and then **History**).
2. You will see every commit your editor made, with their name and the date.
3. Click any commit to see exactly what they changed — additions are shown in green, removals in red.
4. If you disagree with a change, you can edit the file yourself and commit your own version, or leave a comment asking about it.

## Part D: Removing an editor

When the editing is finished:

1. Go to **Settings** > **Collaborators**.
2. Find your editor in the list.
3. Click **Remove** next to their name.

They will no longer be able to access your repository, but your files and history remain unchanged.

## Tips for smooth collaboration

- **Agree on a workflow.** Decide whether the editor will make direct changes or leave comments for you to apply.
- **Use commit messages.** When your editor saves changes, ask them to write a short message describing what they did.
- **Resolve comments.** When a discussion is finished, click **Resolve conversation** so you both know it is addressed.
- **Keep backups.** Even though GitHub saves versions, it is still wise to keep your own copy of important manuscripts on your computer or an external drive.

## No secrets

Do not share your GitHub password with your editor. Invite them as a collaborator instead. Never put your editor's personal contact information in a file committed to the repository.
